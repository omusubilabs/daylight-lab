Why the north goes dark

Tampere sits at 61.5°N. That number on its own does not explain the winter. Latitude decides how far north you are; it does not decide that December is dark.

The tilt does that. Earth’s axis leans 23.44° away from the perpendicular of its orbit, and it keeps pointing at the same distant spot all year. As the planet goes around, the Sun’s angle above the equator swings with it. That angle is the declination: 23.44° north in June, 23.44° south in December.

Declination decides how much of the Sun’s daily circle clears your horizon. On December 21 at 61.5°N, very little of it does. The Sun rises at 09:42, climbs to 5.1° above the horizon, and sets again at 15:03. That is 5h 21m of daylight.

Press No tilt (0°). Declination stays at zero all year and the chart flattens. Tampere then gets 12h 14m of daylight on every date, December and June within five seconds of each other.

Noon is not at 12:00

Solar noon is the moment the Sun is highest. It is almost never at 12:00, for two unrelated reasons.

The first is political. Japan runs on one time zone, anchored to 135°E, and Niigata is at 139.0°E — four degrees east of its own clock. The Sun crosses Niigata’s meridian about 16 minutes before the clock reaches noon. Tampere has the opposite problem. It sits at 23.8°E on a zone anchored to 30°E, so its solar noon lands late: 12:23 on December 21.

The second is orbital. Earth’s orbit is an ellipse and its axis is tilted. Together those run the Sun ahead of or behind the mean clock by up to about a quarter of an hour. That is the equation of time, and it is why Niigata’s solar noon is not parked at 11:44 but wanders between 11:27 and 11:58 over the year.

Switch the clock control to Solar time to take both effects out. Solar noon then sits at exactly 12:00 on every date, and the daylight band becomes symmetric around it.

White nights

Scrub to June 21 and look at Tampere. The Sun sets at 23:11 and rises again at 03:41, four and a half hours later. In between it does not go far down. At its lowest it is 5.1° below the horizon.

Civil twilight ends at 6° below. Tampere never gets there. From June 5 to July 7, 33 nights running, the sky stays inside civil twilight until sunrise and the chart never reaches its night color. That is what white nights are: not midnight sun, but a night that never finishes darkening.

None of this needs the Arctic Circle, which is another 5° north at 66.6°N, some 560 km away. Tampere gets no midnight sun on any date of the year.

Niigata on the same night is unremarkable. Its Sun drops to 28.7° below the horizon and the night gets properly dark.

Methods and limits

Sun positions use the NOAA solar calculator’s formulation, accurate to roughly a minute for sunrise and sunset at mid-latitudes. Every figure here is computed in your browser. Nothing is fetched from a server.

Moving the slider away from 23.44° gives a teaching model, not a physically self-consistent alternate Earth. The small nutation and aberration terms in the NOAA series were fitted to the real Earth and are kept unchanged. At 23.44° the numbers match NOAA. Away from it, read the shape rather than the minutes.

Refraction is a fixed 0.833° allowance, with no correction for pressure or temperature. The Sun meets the horizon at a shallower angle the further you go from the equator, so that allowance adds about 7 minutes to the day at the equator, 14 minutes at Tampere and 19 at Tromsø. It is why a 0° tilt gives 12h 14m rather than exactly 12h.

Observer elevation is ignored; everything is computed at sea level. Time zone rules, daylight saving included, come from your browser’s own database. The reference year is fixed at 2026 so that a shared link always shows the same year.
